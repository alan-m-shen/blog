Every revolution begins with a promise. On Manor Farm, that promise is a life in which animals no longer work for someone else’s benefit. By the final chapter, equality survives only as a slogan: “All animals are equal, but some animals are more equal than others.”[^equality]

What stays with me is how gradually that betrayal becomes ordinary. The pigs do not announce that the revolution has failed. They keep its vocabulary and change what the words permit.

## Who Gets to Remember?

Snowball’s role in the rebellion is rewritten, and Squealer’s explanations repeatedly ask the animals to distrust their own memories. The commandments change too, while the animals struggle to establish what the wall used to say.[^memory] Power becomes harder to challenge when the evidence of its promises can be edited by the people who benefit from breaking them.

The animals are not simply foolish. Snowball attempts to teach them to read, with uneven results; later, the sheep’s chanting helps drown out debate.[^education] I read these scenes as a reminder that access to words and the freedom to question them matter together. A written rule offers little protection if no one can safely insist on its meaning.

## The Final Betrayal

Boxer’s fate makes the cost personal. After a lifetime of work, he is taken away in a van bearing a horse slaughterer’s name. Squealer supplies a reassuring explanation, and the chapter closes with the pigs acquiring more whisky.[^boxer] The juxtaposition is devastating: loyalty has made Boxer useful, but it has not made him secure.

Orwell’s target was historically specific. In his 1947 preface to the Ukrainian edition, he described challenging the Soviet myth as necessary to renewing socialism.[^preface] The novel nevertheless leaves me with a wider question: what protects a movement’s promises once its leaders control the account of whether those promises have been kept?

The closing scene offers no comforting answer. Pig and human become indistinguishable to the animals watching from outside.[^ending] The rulers have changed, but the governed still stand on the other side of the window.
