Milton sets out to “justify the ways of God to men,” yet Satan is often the character who holds my attention.[^purpose] He has the energy of someone who refuses to accept defeat. That makes him compelling; it does not make him right.

This tension is what I enjoy most about *Paradise Lost*. A poem can invite us to listen closely to a speaker while leaving us uneasy about the uses of his eloquence.

## The Appeal of Defiance

“Better to reign in Hell, than serve in Heaven” sounds like a declaration of independence.[^reign] But its ambition is also revealing. Satan wants to reign. His rejection of subordination does not necessarily amount to a rejection of hierarchy.

For me, the line captures how easily the language of freedom can become the language of personal power. It is possible to admire the force of a sentence before stopping to ask what kind of world its speaker proposes.

The confidence also has cracks. Alone in book 4, Satan admits that he carries Hell within himself, then recoils from the submission that repentance would require.[^hell] His public grandeur sits beside a private inability to change. That contrast makes him more interesting than either a straightforward hero or a villain without an inner life.

## Rebellion and Its Limits

Milton’s republican commitments make the poem’s treatment of authority especially intriguing. But the political parallels need care: as Cordelia Zukerman and Thomas H. Luxon explain, critics disagree about how Satan’s rhetoric relates to Milton’s politics and about whether earthly monarchy can be compared with divine rule.[^politics] Reading Satan as a simple portrait of Cromwell would close down a question that the poem keeps open.

Free will presents a similar tension. In book 3, God insists that his creatures could have remained obedient and that genuine allegiance requires freedom.[^freedom] The capacity to fall matters; it does not follow that the Fall had to happen, or that evil was necessary for goodness to exist.

That distinction keeps my sympathy for Satan from becoming agreement with him. His voice makes rebellion attractive, while his choices expose what attraction can conceal. The poem leaves me listening twice: first to the speech, then to the ambition behind it.
