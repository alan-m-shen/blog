        Sympathy for the Devil? Why We Can’t Stop Talking About Milton’s Satan
lastmod: 2026-10-13
---

Forget the devil as a simple monster. John Milton's Satan has the voice and presence of an epic hero: eloquent in defeat, defiant before overwhelming power, and determined to make his followers believe that their cause can survive. In *Paradise Lost*, first published in 1667, Milton set out to “justify the ways of God to men.”[^purpose] Yet the rebel against God can command a reader's attention as forcefully as the order he opposes.

That tension makes Satan worth returning to. How does a Christian poem give rebellion such persuasive energy without making rebellion right? The question runs through Satan's speeches, Milton's political world, and the poem's account of free will.

## The Rebel You're Supposed to Hate

Satan speaks as a leader who refuses to let defeat determine his worth. “Better to reign in Hell, than serve in Heaven” turns exile into a declaration of independence.[^reign] The line has the force of a revolutionary slogan: even the worst territory seems preferable to submission.

But the word “reign” complicates that appeal. Satan objects to being ruled while imagining freedom as his own opportunity to rule. His courage and eloquence do not settle whether the cause they serve deserves admiration. Milton allows the heroic pose to attract us, then gives us reasons to question it.

The private Satan is less secure than the public speaker. In book 4, he recognizes that he carries Hell within himself and considers what repentance would require, only to recoil from submission.[^hell] His suffering gives the rebellion a tragic dimension: he can recognize his condition without accepting the change that might end it. Pride keeps him attached to the identity his speeches have created.

This makes him more disturbing than a villain who merely enjoys evil. The familiar human impulses—wounded dignity, ambition, the refusal to admit defeat—help explain his appeal. They also show how an attractive account of oneself can become a reason to persist in doing harm.

## A Revolutionary for Revolutionary Times

Milton knew a world in which the language of liberty could overturn a political order. He defended the English republic and served as a government secretary, continuing under Cromwell's Protectorate.[^politics] Questions about obedience, legitimate authority, and the fate of a revolution were part of his public life, not just material for an imagined war in Heaven.

The parallel with Satan is therefore tempting. A leader challenges a sovereign, claims a righteous cause, and risks reproducing the power he condemns. Cromwell's career gives that pattern a historical resonance. But it would be too simple to make Satan a disguised portrait of Cromwell or to assume that the poem records Milton's rejection of him. Milton's attitude to the Protectorate remains a matter of scholarly disagreement; Tobias Gregory, for example, challenges the familiar narrative of straightforward disillusionment.[^cromwell]

The comparison is useful as a question rather than a key that unlocks every detail. What distinguishes resistance to tyranny from a struggle for personal supremacy? Can the rhetoric of freedom tell us which we are hearing? Those questions connect Milton's political experience to Satan's appeal without requiring divine authority and earthly monarchy to be the same thing.

## The Freedom to Choose Damnation

Satan's persuasiveness also matters to the poem's treatment of free will. If obedience were unavoidable, it could hardly express loyalty. In book 3, God describes his creatures as “Sufficient to have stood, though free to fall.”[^freedom] Their freedom includes the possibility of disobedience, but the Fall is not presented as a necessary lesson they had to undergo before moral choice became possible.

The temptation makes that freedom dramatically difficult. Satan presents the forbidden fruit to Eve as a route to knowledge and a higher existence. Adam's choice is different: the narrator says he eats despite knowing better, rather than because Satan's argument has deceived him.[^temptation] The two decisions give the Fall more weight than a story in which everyone is simply overpowered by an irresistible villain. Persuasion, attachment, and judgment all have a part in the disaster.

Milton's grand language and blank verse give these conflicts their scale, but Satan's appeal keeps them uncomfortably close. We can feel the force of his defiance while seeing the ambition it conceals. Sympathy does not have to end in acquittal. The poem makes room for both the attraction of rebellion and the responsibility that remains when a person chooses it.
