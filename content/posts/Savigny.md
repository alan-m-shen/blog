Imagine buying a jacket from a seller abroad. The buyer, seller, payment, and delivery may all connect the transaction to different countries. If something goes wrong, where should we begin when deciding which law applies?

Savigny offers an appealing way of organizing the question: look for the legal relationship’s “seat.” In the eighth volume of his *System of Modern Roman Law*, translated into English as *Private International Law*, he asks which legal territory a relationship belongs to by its nature.[^seat] I think of this as a legal GPS, though the metaphor works better as a starting point than as a promise of an automatic answer.

## Finding a Home

Savigny considers connections such as a person’s domicile, the location of property, and the place of a legal act. He also criticizes the inherited division of statutes into personal, real, and mixed categories.[^connections] The attractive move is to begin with the relationship we are trying to understand, rather than simply insisting that the court’s own law must govern everything before it.

Contracts show why this remains difficult. An obligation is not a physical object with an obvious address. Savigny gives particular importance to the place of performance and the parties’ expectations.[^contracts] Even on that account, deciding where a relationship belongs requires interpretation. The map does not draw itself.

## The Promise—and the Qualification

There is an appealing aspiration behind the method: the outcome should not depend simply on which country hears the case. Savigny connects that aspiration to reciprocity and a developing community of law.[^community] It asks a court to take a foreign connection seriously, even when applying its own rules would be more familiar.

That vision needs qualification. His account invokes a shared Christian morality, and he expressly recognizes limits to the application of foreign laws.[^limits] It would be too generous to describe this as an uncomplicated theory of universal equality among every legal system.

What I find useful is the habit of thought. Before reaching for a familiar rule, ask what relationships and places actually matter to the dispute. The difficult part is deciding why one connection should count more than another. Calling a relationship’s home “natural” can make that judgment sound easier than it is.
