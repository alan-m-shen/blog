Imagine you're in Canada and you buy a handmade leather jacket from a small shop in Italy. The payment is processed through a bank in Ireland, and the jacket is shipped from a warehouse in Germany. It arrives damaged. You decide to sue. But whose law applies? Canada's? Italy's? Germany's?

This puzzle belongs to private international law. The nineteenth-century German jurist Friedrich Carl von Savigny proposed a systematic way to approach it: identify the legal relationship, then find its “seat,” the legal territory to which it belongs by its nature.[^seat] Think of it as a legal GPS. Its value lies in explaining which connections matter, rather than merely counting how many countries appear in the story.

## The Old Way: A Labyrinth of Rules

The inherited “statute theory” began by classifying laws. Were they personal, concerning a person's status; real, concerning property; or mixed, concerning acts or some combination of the two? The classification was supposed to determine a law's territorial reach.

The trouble was that the categories did not settle their own boundaries. A rule could concern both a person and property, and writers disagreed about what belonged in each class. Savigny criticized the scheme as incomplete and ambiguous.[^statutes] His alternative changed the starting point: instead of asking how far a particular law should reach, ask which law should govern a particular relationship.

## Savigny's Breakthrough: Every Problem Has a Home

In the eighth volume of his *System of Modern Roman Law*, translated into English as *Private International Law*, Savigny developed this search for the seat of a legal relationship. The metaphor is simple, but the method is not a rule that everything follows the same address. Different relationships call for different connections:

- **Personal status:** domicile supplies the starting point for matters such as a person's capacity to hold rights. This is a legal connection to a home, not simply wherever someone happens to be staying.[^status]
- **Rights in tangible property:** the location of the thing supplies the seat. A dispute over rights in a piece of land has a territorial connection that is difficult to ignore.[^property]
- **Contractual obligations:** the place of performance and the parties' intentions become central. An obligation has no physical location, so finding its seat requires more interpretation than locating a field or a house.[^contracts]

Return to the jacket. The route taken by the payment does not automatically determine the law governing the seller's obligation. We first need to identify the issue and explain why a particular connection should govern it. Savigny's method offers a reasoned way to make that choice, even where the court ultimately applies foreign law. It does not promise that the facts will announce a single answer without legal judgment.

## A Global Vision for Justice

Behind the method lies an ambitious ideal: the same legal relationship should receive the same decision whether the case is heard in one country or another. A dispute should not change its legal character simply because the claimant finds a different courtroom. Savigny linked this aspiration to reciprocity and a developing legal community; applying foreign law should be more dependable than an occasional courtesy.[^community]

That is a powerful argument against automatically favouring the court's own law, but its historical limits matter. Savigny invoked a shared Christian morality and recognized exceptions to the application of foreign law, including certain mandatory rules and institutions the forum would not recognize.[^limits] His universalism was an aspiration toward consistent treatment across borders, not an unqualified declaration that every legal system and every rule were interchangeable.

## Savigny's Legacy and the Puzzles That Remain

Savigny's question survives in later efforts to identify a relationship's closest or most significant connection. Sagi Peari, for example, links Savigny's idea of voluntary submission to the “most significant relationship” principle in the American Second Restatement.[^legacy] The lasting idea is that the court's location alone does not settle which law should govern.

The harder problem comes before we choose a connecting factor: how do we decide what kind of legal question we are looking at? Imagine that one legal system treats a broken promise to marry as a contractual matter and another treats it as a civil wrong. The same facts could then lead toward different choice-of-law rules. This is the problem of characterization. A later English judgment, *Macmillan v. Bishopsgate*, makes the sequence explicit: characterize the issue, select the connecting rule, and identify the applicable law. It also warns against letting domestic categories distort the inquiry.[^characterization]

Here the GPS metaphor reaches its limit. Before the system can give directions, someone must decide what the destination represents. Calling a relationship's seat “natural” cannot remove that interpretive step. Savigny's achievement was to give cross-border disputes a coherent method, not to eliminate the judgments the method requires. His question remains useful: where does this relationship belong, and what makes that connection the right one?
